## Recommendation

Keep the Go/PostgreSQL/React monolith and one application replica. Before a public launch, fix the contract blockers in [the skeptic review](skeptic-architecture.md), bound application work, and document anonymous voting honestly. Treat existing provider protection as a network baseline with an unverified capacity, rather than claiming complete DDoS protection.

For stronger availability, evaluate an external HTTP reverse proxy with Russian connectivity, using **DDoS-Guard and StormWall as the first practical comparisons** and **Qrator/Curator as a conditional alternative**. This ordering is an inference from documented subdomain management and integration constraints, not a performance ranking. No purchase is recommended without a quote, a no-CAPTCHA configuration, a privacy review of TLS termination, and a trial from the intended Russian networks. Keep Cloudflare proxying out of the critical path for a Russia-first service unless new access evidence changes the assessment.

Centralize policy and vendor administration for selected public `*.kreoniz.ru` web applications, but inventory and onboard each hostname explicitly. Wildcard DNS, wildcard TLS certificates, unlimited subdomain licensing, and a single origin firewall rule are different capabilities. None follows automatically from buying protection for `kreoniz.ru`.

## Existing VDSina baseline and its evidence limits

VDSina's official answer says L3/L4 DDoS protection is included. Its staff follow-up of **2025-08-15** says filtering is automatic, places L7 mitigation on the customer, and declines to publish detailed technical parameters. The main site also advertises included DDoS protection. These are provider claims, not evidence that this account has an enhanced service or a particular attack capacity. [VDSina support answer](https://vdsina.ru/qa/q/est-li-zashchita-vds-ot-ddos-atak), [VDSina service site](https://vdsina.ru/).

**Inference:** retaining the present provider is reasonable for a small service accepting some outage risk, but included network filtering does not establish an L7 HTTP-flood service, a guaranteed response time, an attack-size allowance, or protection for every protocol. No current public VDSina source found in this research verified a separately priced enhanced L3/L4 or L7 add-on. This is an evidence gap, not proof that no optional product exists.

Before buying or relying on an optional tier, obtain written confirmation for the actual location/account: covered IPv4/IPv6 addresses, always-on versus triggered filtering, attack and clean-traffic limits, null-route/suspension policy, response process, SLA exclusions, and whether any L7 option is a reverse proxy with separate TLS and client-IP requirements. Do not invent a bandwidth guarantee or a price from a generic VPS plan.

## External reverse-proxy candidates

### StormWall

StormWall advertises website protection covering network/session attacks and L7 floods. Its current site lists filtering locations in **Moscow and Saint Petersburg** and describes a classic connection in which a DNS record points to a protected address; it separately describes BGP protection. This establishes a relevant Russian presence and a compatible service shape, not measured reachability from Omsk or every mobile ISP. [StormWall official product and network overview](https://stormwall.pro/) (undated; checked 2026-10-21).

The official object-management instructions dated **2026-05-18** document multiple backends, HTTPS/WSS and SNI settings, uploaded certificates or managed Let's Encrypt issuance, and static caching. The SSL instructions require certificate/key material for inspection; separate marketing describes protection without disclosing private keys. Do not treat these modes as equivalent HTTP inspection. Confirm the exact termination mode, origin certificate verification, supported hostnames, and API behavior in the quote. [StormWall object configuration](https://stormwall.pro/resources/instructions/website-protection/object).

**Assessment:** viable shortlist candidate for several HTTP applications. Confirm subdomain licensing and limits, current edge source networks, browser checks without CAPTCHA, and path-specific cache bypass. The old 2020 PDF price examples are unsuitable for budgeting in 2026 and are deliberately not reproduced.

### DDoS-Guard

Its first-party documentation describes an HTTP reverse proxy, protected addresses reached through domain/subdomain A records, separate connections to the origin, and optional additional checks for suspicious requests. Additional anti-bot/WAF functions depend on the tariff or modules; do not assume all modules are included in basic DDoS protection. [How DDoS-Guard website protection works](https://ddos-guard.ru/tutorials/website-protection/how-it-works) (undated; checked 2026-10-21).

The subdomain documentation is unusually relevant here: subdomains can share the parent's settings, while explicitly linked subdomains can have independent settings and do not consume the parent-domain count. They must share the protected IP with the parent, have a separate linked-subdomain limit, and **do not inherit settings when added as linked subdomains**. This is useful centralized administration, not unlimited automatic wildcard coverage. [DDoS-Guard flexible subdomains](https://ddos-guard.ru/tutorials/website-protection/flexible-subdomains) (undated; checked 2026-10-21).

Its official migration page claims traffic/data processing in Russia; that is a vendor claim to confirm for the contracted service. Selectel's own service documentation, updated **2026-09-23**, independently confirms that it offers DDoS-Guard L7 protection/CDN and warns that its integration does not protect IPv6. This reseller limitation must not be generalized to every direct DDoS-Guard product. [DDoS-Guard migration page](https://ddos-guard.ru/migration), [Selectel DDoS-Guard documentation](https://docs.selectel.ru/anti-ddos/ddos-guard/).

**Assessment:** first candidate to price for a small group of related hostnames because the per-subdomain control model is documented. Verify Basic versus paid options, origin TLS authentication, subdomain limits, IPv6, all challenge behavior, and API cache bypass. Do not accept a CAPTCHA during attack as an implicit exception to this project's no-CAPTCHA requirement. The public tariff page has prices and feature tiers, but no all-hostnames quote was verified; this document promises no monthly total. [DDoS-Guard website product](https://ddos-guard.ru/web-protection).

### Qrator and the Russia-facing Curator offering

Qrator documents reverse HTTP proxy onboarding by changing the domain's A record and warns that previously published origin IPs can allow protection bypass. Its **2022-01-25** announcement establishes historical Moscow filtering presence; that historical statement alone cannot verify today's topology. The current Russia-facing Curator site offers website/network protection and says CAPTCHA is not used by default. Obtain the quote from the actual contracting entity; do not assume the international Qrator and Curator offerings have identical terms. [Qrator HTTP onboarding](https://docs.qrator.net/connect/reverse-proxies/http.html), [Qrator Moscow announcement](https://qrator.net/news-details/qrator-labs-extends-its-global-ddos-mitigation-network-by-opening-a-new-traffic-filtering-center-in-moscow/), [Curator AntiDDoS](https://curator.pro/solutions/anti-ddos/) (current product page undated).

Both sets of HTTP-proxy documentation explicitly say automatic wildcard certificate issuance is unsupported. Manual certificate upload is described, so this is not evidence that uploaded wildcard certificates or subdomain routing are impossible. More seriously, both say the proxy does **not verify upstream authenticity** for origin TLS. Encryption without endpoint authentication leaves an interception risk. Resolve this with the vendor before selecting that mode for private reports and admin credentials; require verified upstream TLS or a suitably authenticated transport design. [Qrator HTTP proxy technology](https://docs.qrator.net/technologies/reverse-proxies/http.html), [Curator HTTP proxy technology](https://docs.curator.pro/technologies/reverse-proxies/http.html) (undated; checked 2026-10-21).

**Assessment:** credible comparison candidate, with a material integration gate. Confirm the current product, Russian access and routing, named-subdomain coverage, certificate lifecycle, and an authenticated origin path. “No CAPTCHA by default” still needs confirmation for attack mode and any added anti-bot module. No usable project-specific price was verified.

### What “works in Russia” can establish

Russian filtering sites, local commercial availability, and first-party Russian service documentation make these candidates more plausible than an untested foreign edge for this audience. They do **not** prove delivery over each ISP, eliminate blocking/shared-IP risks, or establish good latency. No multi-ISP measurements were performed in this task.

An authorized trial should exercise a named staging hostname from Omsk fixed access and at least two relevant Russian mobile/fixed networks, including ordinary browsers with privacy settings. Check complete JS/CSS downloads larger than 16 KB, report submission, cookie issuance, voting, login/logout, CSRF headers, and 429 behavior. Measure both normal and vendor-approved protection modes; do not generate an attack against shared production infrastructure.

Cloudflare's **2025-06-26** primary report says disruption began on June 9, with roughly 16 KB transfers, connection resets/timeouts, and multiple affected Russian ISPs. It says HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2, and HTTP/3 are affected. This is Cloudflare's measurement and attribution, rather than this reviewer's independent network observation. [Cloudflare incident analysis](https://blog.cloudflare.com/russian-internet-users-are-unable-to-access-the-open-internet/).

Crucially, the official support page, **last updated 2026-04-23**, still warns of systematic throttling and says Cloudflare cannot restore connectivity for Russia-based users through configuration changes. No later primary resolution notice was established in this research. This does not prove every Russian connection fails today, but it is sufficient reason not to make Cloudflare proxy delivery a required dependency for this service. [Cloudflare support: Russian service disruption](https://developers.cloudflare.com/support/troubleshooting/general-troubleshooting/service-disruption/).

An official government notice URL was located through the provider's link, but direct retrieval timed out and then returned an unavailable error: [ЦМУ ССОП notice concerning Cloudflare](https://noc.gov.ru/ru/news/рекомендуем-отказаться-от-cdn-сервиса-cloudflare/). Its text/date was **not verified** and is not used here to claim a legal ban or a particular blocking policy. The provider's interpretation of Russian law is not a substitute for that primary text.

**Recommendation:** self-host runtime JS, fonts, and UI assets; exclude Cloudflare-hosted challenges or other required external browser dependencies from the critical form path. Cloudflare authoritative DNS and Cloudflare proxy/CDN delivery are separate roles; these observations do not justify an unrelated DNS migration. This task proposes no DNS changes.

## What local Caddy and Go controls can actually do

Caddy documents request-body size limits and server header/body/write/idle timeouts. Its proxy trust configuration supports explicit source ranges and strict right-to-left forwarded-address parsing. Such controls reduce work and slow-client exposure after traffic has reached the server; settings and syntax must match the installed version. Caddy's `http.handlers.rate_limit` page describes nonstandard modules, so do not assume a generic rate-limit directive exists in the current shared binary. [Caddy request_body](https://caddyserver.com/docs/caddyfile/directives/request_body), [Caddy server and proxy options](https://caddyserver.com/docs/caddyfile/options#servers), [Caddy rate-limit module notice](https://caddyserver.com/docs/modules/http.handlers.rate_limit) (living documentation; checked 2026-10-21).

Go's `net/http.Server` exposes header/read/write/idle timeouts and header limits; `MaxBytesReader` bounds body reading. Header limits do not bound the body, and timeouts do not automatically bound database work. Apply request contexts, finite database pool/statement time, and admission controls separately. [Go Server](https://pkg.go.dev/net/http#Server), [Go MaxBytesReader](https://pkg.go.dev/net/http#MaxBytesReader) (living standard-library documentation; checked 2026-10-21).

OWASP distinguishes bandwidth saturation from application resource exhaustion and recommends cheap validation before expensive work. **Inference:** rejecting HTTP traffic in Caddy or Go cannot recover an already saturated upstream link. A second ordinary VPS reverse proxy merely moves the exposed bottleneck; upstream/provider scrubbing or a suitable edge is needed for volumetric capacity. Neither a honeypot nor a WAF is a substitute for network capacity, and a network scrubbing service does not fix expensive valid database queries. [OWASP Denial of Service guidance](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Denial_of_Service_Cheat_Sheet.html).

Proposed starting budgets, **not measured capacity or industry guarantees**: JSON bodies 32 KiB; title 200 characters; description 5,000; details 10,000; context fields separately bounded; query/search 200 characters; HTTP header deadline 5 seconds; whole-request deadline 10 seconds; DB work deadline 2 seconds. Start with at most 16 admitted DB-backed handlers, an 8-connection pool, and one concurrent password verification. These are conservative tuning proposals for the saved small shared-host context; validate and adjust on a local disposable database before calling them safe. Never hold a handler asleep to enforce form age; reject quickly with a usable error.

Keep limiter keys and total memory bounded, including unknown feature IDs, missing/invalid cookies, changing IPv6 addresses, and failed logins. Expire entries, impose a hard key cap, and use a bounded overflow response rather than silently permitting unlimited new keys. A one-replica in-memory limiter may reset on restart; DB vote uniqueness must survive. State that tradeoff before adding replicas. A busy public route must not consume every admin/health connection. Monitor aggregate rejection counts, query time, pool wait, disk and queue growth without content or identifier labels.

## Anonymous votes, NAT, and privacy

Browser cookie state can be removed by the user or user agent; an expiry is an upper bound, not durable identity. A signed cookie prevents modifying its contents into a chosen visitor ID, but does not stop obtaining a fresh cookie. [RFC 6265 §§4.1.2 and 7.2](https://httpwg.org/specs/rfc6265.html) (April 2011). Shared public IPs also fail as personal identity; RFC 6269 explains collateral blocking under address sharing. [RFC 6269 §13.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6269.html#section-13.1) (June 2011).

**Proposed policy:** one counted vote per issued visitor identity and visible feature, enforced by a database unique constraint. An existing vote retry returns success before consuming a new-vote cooldown. For new votes, use a short, explicitly documented per-network/per-feature cooldown (a starting proposal is 60 seconds) plus separate endpoint/global admission limits. Reserve the cooldown atomically with vote insertion; roll it back on failed writes. Cookie reset within that window meets friction; reset after it, another browser/device, proxies, and distributed clients can still add votes. Do not describe this as preventing reset-based vote inflation in general.

The tradeoff is real: a second student on university Wi-Fi or a mobile CGNAT may be delayed by someone else's vote; a person switching networks may bypass the delay. Longer IP bans or a permanent unique IP/report constraint penalize shared networks more severely without establishing identity. Use anonymous counts as a weak prioritization signal, independently of criticality and information quality.

**Privacy proposal:** derive short-lived network limiter keys with a dedicated keyed HMAC over a canonical address (IPv4-mapped IPv6 normalized); do not use an unkeyed IP hash with its enumerable IPv4 input space. Exact address is simplest; coarsening IPv6 to /64 is an optional abuse tradeoff requiring explicit documentation because it joins users and is not a personal identifier either. Keep network tags in memory with a hard maximum lifetime, e.g. 24 hours; rotate the key with a defined overlap only if needed to preserve an active cooldown. Do not put network tags into permanent vote/report rows. Keep the application-specific visitor identity separate from the admin session and from identities on sibling services. Set and document a cookie lifetime, e.g. 90 days; expiry/reset may permit another vote. These are proposed engineering choices, not claims of legal anonymization. Persistent identifiers can correlate activity, even when pseudonymous. [RFC 6973 §§5.2.1 and 6.1](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6973.html) (July 2013).

Suggested Russian disclosure, adjusted to implemented retention before release:

> Вход не нужен. Голос запоминается в cookie этого браузера. Мы временно ограничиваем частые действия с одного сетевого адреса; это может затронуть общую сеть. Очистка cookie, другой браузер или устройство могут позволить проголосовать снова. Счётчик не означает число уникальных людей.

Avoid “fully anonymous”: private report text, retained visitor identifiers, and a TLS-terminating protection provider can still create linkage. Publish concrete retention for reports, votes, session state, moderation metadata, backups, and any edge logs; clearing a cookie does not delete already stored reports or votes.

## Abuse friction without CAPTCHA

OWASP describes automation as distinct from DDoS and notes the weaknesses of IP-only and cookie-only limits. Its layered approach informs the following design, but all specific thresholds here remain application proposals. [OWASP Bot Management and Anti-Automation](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Bot_Management_and_Anti-Automation_Cheat_Sheet.html) (living guidance; checked 2026-10-21).

Keep the `website` honeypot noninteractive for keyboard/screen-reader users and away from autofill. Populating it should return an indistinguishable accepted receipt with no stored report. It catches simple form fillers; a bot reading the form will omit it. Do not ban an address or visitor solely because the field is filled. Test autofill and assistive technology so this cheap trap does not silently discard legitimate reports.

Bind the signed form token to the issued visitor cookie, its purpose, and server issuance time; enforce the two-second age and two-hour expiry server-side. A fresh token response needs `Cache-Control: no-store`. A bot can wait two seconds, reuse a still-valid token unless constrained, and obtain many tokens; this is friction, not proof of humanity. Exact normalization/deduplication reduces duplicate rows; do not disclose cross-visitor private matches. Use per-cookie, per-network, and endpoint budgets, a bounded moderation backlog, and an operator read-only/submission-pause switch for overload. Preserve clear retry behavior for legitimate users. Do not add fingerprinting, automatic language rejection, or registration to compensate for missing identity.

## Proxy trust, CSRF, XSS, and admin sessions

Use one authoritative client-IP derivation. With direct public Caddy, ignore visitor-supplied forwarded headers. With an external edge, Caddy should trust only that edge's documented source ranges, parse append-style chains from the right, and remove/replace the canonical client-IP header passed to the app. The app accepts that header only from its exact intended Caddy peer/network; otherwise it uses the socket peer. `private_ranges`, every Docker bridge, and `0.0.0.0/0` are not adequate substitutes for a known trust boundary. [Caddy trusted proxies](https://caddyserver.com/docs/caddyfile/options#trusted-proxies).

OWASP recommends origin checks and session-bound CSRF protection and warns that sibling origins may remain same-site. **Application policy:** compare the parsed scheme/host/port to configured `PUBLIC_ORIGIN`, reject `Origin: null`, and require Origin or an exact-origin Referer fallback for browser mutations; do not accept a hostname suffix. Apply to login, logout, reports and votes. Keep JSON content type strict and cross-origin credentialed access disabled. Require the admin CSRF header as well. Go's `CrossOriginProtection` can help, but its documented defaults permit requests with neither relevant header and compare hostname information; it alone does not implement this stricter contract. [OWASP CSRF guidance](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Cross-Site_Request_Forgery_Prevention_Cheat_Sheet.html), [Go CrossOriginProtection](https://pkg.go.dev/net/http#CrossOriginProtection).

For admin sessions, OWASP supports unpredictable opaque IDs, server-held state, renewal and server-side expiry. **Proposal:** 32 random bytes, store only a token hash plus session/CSRF state, rotate on login, revoke on logout/password change, enforce 30-minute idle and 8-hour absolute expiry, bound active sessions, and keep password verification behind its own admission control. Use host-only `__Host-` cookies with Secure, HttpOnly, Path=/ and SameSite=Strict in production; use explicitly separate development settings. Never reuse the anonymous cookie as authentication or put session/CSRF tokens in localStorage or URLs. No-store all admin API responses, including login failures. [OWASP Session Management](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Session_Management_Cheat_Sheet.html).

Render all report content and admin notes as escaped text, including private admin previews. An admin-only stored XSS payload can act with the owner's session even if the cookie is HttpOnly. Do not introduce Markdown/raw HTML or automatically turn context into executable URLs. Deploy a self-only CSP compatible with the built assets, frame restrictions and `nosniff` as defense in depth; verify the actual policy in a browser. [OWASP XSS prevention](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Cross_Site_Scripting_Prevention_Cheat_Sheet.html).

## A shared edge needs a separate infrastructure decision

An HTTP proxy protects only traffic sent through it. Historical DNS and an unproxied sibling hostname may reveal the same shared origin IP. Qrator explicitly recommends addressing this bypass risk. [Qrator domain onboarding warning](https://docs.qrator.net/dashboard/domains.html).

**Inference for the saved shared-Caddy topology:** allowing only edge IPs to the entire shared 443 listener would also affect unrelated sites. A host-specific Caddy deny rule can stop a direct HTTP request to Feedback Hub but cannot prevent packets saturating the public link. Do not claim that changing one hostname's DNS hides or protects the shared VPS from direct network attacks. Later options include routing all affected public HTTP hosts through the approved edge, using a separate suitably protected origin address, or arranging a supported authenticated tunnel. Provider network mitigation remains relevant in every option. Each requires an explicit, separately authorized migration plan.

TLS inspection also adds the protection provider to the trusted parties that can see bug descriptions, admin login requests and session cookies. Confirm processing locations, origin authentication, log/body/cookie collection, retention, support access, incident reporting and deletion before enabling it. “HTTPS on both legs” is insufficient if certificates are not validated. A no-key-disclosure mode may have different L7 capabilities; obtain a precise description instead of treating the marketing label as equivalent protection.

For the first edge configuration, bypass caching for **all `/api/*` and admin HTML**, respect no-store, and cache only versioned public assets. `Feature.voted`, form tokens, and sessions are visitor-specific. Do not enable POST retries that can create additional work; retain server idempotence anyway. Preserve Host/SNI, Origin, CSRF headers, cookies, 429 and Retry-After. Confirm IPv6 and eliminate any unprotected alternate path as part of the authorized plan. Keep private management services outside an indiscriminate wildcard onboarding.

## Phased plan and decision gates

### Phase 0 — finish the application safely, without infrastructure changes

Resolve the numbered contract findings in the skeptic review with backend/frontend owners. Pick and document actual budgets, retention, voting cooldown, cookie/session lifetime, generic dedupe receipts, visibility predicates, and cache policy. Verify the narrow security invariants against a disposable PostgreSQL instance and local browser. Record results separately from this research. This research is not the later independent implementation review.

### Phase 1 — establish a measured provider-only baseline

For any later authorized launch, verify the selected VDSina account's protection and escalation process, production HTTPS/proxy trust, resource limits, backup restore, and absence of private data in app/Caddy/access/error logs. Keep one replica. Use aggregate metrics and normal low-volume health checks; test ordinary Russian delivery. Acknowledge that a distributed L7 attack or direct volumetric attack may still cause an outage. If that availability risk is unacceptable, Phase 2 becomes a pre-launch dependency rather than a future enhancement.

### Phase 2 — request comparable quotes and run a controlled trial

Ask DDoS-Guard and StormWall for the same explicit list of public hostnames and clean-traffic assumptions; ask Curator/Qrator only with the origin-authentication question attached. Require written terms covering subdomain count, TLS/renewal, verified upstream transport, no CAPTCHA including attack mode, API pass-through, IPv4/IPv6, clean-traffic/request limits, attack charges, support response and SLA exclusions. No costs are assumed here beyond VDSina's published included baseline. Trial a dedicated staging hostname only after authorization. Preserve DNS ownership and review certificate issuance before any cutover; managed certificates may require DNS already pointing to the edge.

### Phase 3 — separately approve a staged shared-edge rollout

Prepare a concrete per-host routing/certificate plan, current edge source networks, origin bypass treatment compatible with other services, cache exclusions, privacy/log policy, DNS TTL and rollback conditions. Obtain explicit authorization for DNS/firewall/shared-Caddy changes only after that plan is reviewable. Migrate one selected host at a time and verify normal traffic and privacy invariants before expanding. A DNS rollback may restore origin exposure and depends on resolver caches; do not call it instant or risk-free.

### Phase 4 — respond to evidence

Use observed rejection/false-positive rates, moderation backlog and resource pressure to tune limits. During abuse, pause writes or reduce expensive reads while preserving health/admin access. Buy stronger upstream protection if availability requires it; add authenticated voting only if the product actually needs stronger identity. Keep voting quality independent of popularity. Recheck vendor terms and Russian reachability before purchase or renewal: the findings above are dated evidence, not standing deployment authorization.
